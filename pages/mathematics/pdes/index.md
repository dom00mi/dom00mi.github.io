# Partial Differential Equations

## From Fundamental Equations to Advanced PDE Theory

***Intent.*** These notes are written primarily for educational purposes, with the hope that they may also be useful to anyone interested in exploring the mathematical foundations of partial differential equations.

Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) occupy a central place in mathematics, providing a rigorous language for describing how quantities evolve, interact, and vary across space and time. From the diffusion of heat to the propagation of waves, from fluid dynamics to mathematical finance, PDEs offer a remarkable connection between abstract mathematical reasoning and the physical world.

Their importance is not merely practical. The study of PDEs has motivated some of the deepest developments in modern mathematics, bringing together ideas from Analysis, Geometry, Functional Analysis, and Numerical Mathematics.

As **Felix Klein** observed in *The Evanston Colloquium* (1893), the study of transcendental functions defined by differential equations represented a central problem of modern mathematics.

This perspective reflects the profound role differential equations have played in shaping mathematical thought.

The contributions of **Peter D. Lax** and **Louis Nirenberg** further illustrate the depth of the subject. Their work transformed our understanding of partial differential equations, including questions of existence, regularity, stability, and the mathematical methods used to investigate solutions.


**Peter D. Lax** was awarded the 2005 Abel Prize:

> "for his groundbreaking contributions to the theory and application of partial differential equations and to the computation of their solutions."

**Louis Nirenberg** received the 2015 Abel Prize jointly with John Nash for their contributions to the theory of nonlinear partial differential equations and its applications to geometric analysis.


There is also something particularly fascinating about the relationship between differential equations and the conditions imposed upon their solutions.

As **Alan Turing** famously wrote:

> "Science is a differential equation. Religion is a boundary condition."
>
> Ã¢â‚¬â€ Alan Turing, postcard to Robin Gandy (1954)

Beyond its philosophical meaning, the remark provides a memorable reminder that a differential equation alone may not determine a unique solution. Initial and boundary conditions are often essential in identifying the particular solution relevant to a given problem.

> "In order to solve this differential equation you look at it till a solution occurs to you."

Behind the humour lies an important mathematical lesson: finding a solution is not always a mechanical process, that trascends several areas of mathematics. Intuition, observation, and a careful understanding of the underlying structure often play an essential role.

With these ideas in mind, we will revisit the theory of Partial Differential Equations from its foundations, beginning with their definitions and elementary classifications before gradually progressing towards the analysis of more advanced equations.

Our aim is not simply to learn how to solve PDEs, but also to understand **why solutions exist, when they are unique, how regular they are, and what their behaviour tells us about the phenomena they describe**.

I hope you enjoy the journey.

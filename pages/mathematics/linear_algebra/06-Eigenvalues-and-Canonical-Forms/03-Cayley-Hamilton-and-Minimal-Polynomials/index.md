# 6.3 — Cayley–Hamilton and Minimal Polynomials

The characteristic polynomial of a matrix records its eigenvalues, but it also yields a striking identity involving the matrix itself. The **Cayley–Hamilton theorem** states that every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial. The **minimal polynomial** then finds the polynomial identity of lowest possible degree and detects the obstruction to diagonalisation.

Throughout this section let $A\in M_n(K)$ and write

$$
\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)=t^n+a_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1t+a_0.
$$

## 6.3.1 — Polynomials evaluated at a matrix or operator

**Definition 6.3.1.** For

$$
p(t)=c_dt^d+\cdots+c_1t+c_0\in K[t],
$$

its evaluation at $A$ is

$$
\boxed{p(A)=c_dA^d+\cdots+c_1A+c_0I_n.}
$$

Similarly, for an operator $T:V\to V$ set $p(T)=c_dT^d+\cdots+c_0I_V$. If $A$ represents $T$ in a basis, then $p(A)$ represents $p(T)$ in the same basis. Polynomials obey the ordinary rules

$$
(p+q)(A)=p(A)+q(A),\qquad
(pq)(A)=p(A)q(A),
$$

and $p(A)q(A)=q(A)p(A)$: both factors are polynomials in the *same* matrix, even though arbitrary matrices do not commute.

**Example 6.3.2.** Let

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\chi_A(t)=(t-2)(t-3)=t^2-5t+6.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\chi_A(A)&=A^2-5A+6I\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&9\end{pmatrix}
-\begin{pmatrix}10&0\\0&15\end{pmatrix}
+\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\0&6\end{pmatrix}\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

This vanishing is not special to diagonal matrices.

## 6.3.2 — Cayley–Hamilton theorem

**Theorem 6.3.3 (Cayley–Hamilton).** Every square matrix over a field satisfies its characteristic polynomial:

$$
\boxed{\chi_A(A)=A^n+a_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1A+a_0I=0.}
$$

The same conclusion holds for a matrix over any commutative ring.

### Proof I: the adjugate identity

For any square matrix $C$, we have

$$
C\operatorname{adj}(C)=\det(C)I.
$$

Apply this to the **polynomial matrix** $tI-A$ (with entries in the commutative ring $K[t]$):

$$
(tI-A)\operatorname{adj}(tI-A)=\chi_A(t)I.
\tag{6.3.1}
$$

Because each cofactor is a polynomial in $t$ of degree at most $n-1$, write

$$
\operatorname{adj}(tI-A)=B_{n-1}t^{n-1}+B_{n-2}t^{n-2}+\cdots+B_1t+B_0,
$$

with $B_j\in M_n(K)$. On multiplying and comparing coefficients of equal powers of $t$ in (6.3.1), we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
B_{n-1}&=I,\\
B_{n-2}-AB_{n-1}&=a_{n-1}I,\\
B_{n-3}-AB_{n-2}&=a_{n-2}I,\\
&\ \vdots\\
B_0-AB_1&=a_1I,\\
-AB_0&=a_0I.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.3.2}
$$

Multiply these identities **on the left** by $A^n,A^{n-1},A^{n-2},\ldots,A,I$, respectively, and add. Every intermediate $A^jB_k$ cancels, leaving

$$
0=A^n+a_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1A+a_0I=\chi_A(A).
$$

This is the desired result. $\square$

**Important caution.** It is *not* a valid proof to write $\chi_A(A)=\det(AI-A)=\det(0)=0$: the variable $t$ in $\det(tI-A)$ is a **scalar indeterminate**, and naively replacing it by a matrix inside a determinant is ill-defined. The proof above works precisely because the adjugate identity is first established in $M_n(K[t])$, and the resulting matrix-coefficient polynomial identity is then handled rigorously.

### Proof II: invariant flags and triangularisation

We give the structural proof appearing in the second set of sources. If $K$ is algebraically closed (or after passing to an algebraic closure), §6.2 gives a basis in which $A$ is upper triangular, say $U$, with diagonal entries $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$. Let

$$
0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n=K^n,
\qquad V_j=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\ldots,e_j).
$$

Upper triangularity implies

$$
(U-\lambda_jI)V_j\subseteq V_{j-1}.
$$

Therefore the successive commuting factors

$$
(U-\lambda_1I)(U-\lambda_2I)\cdots(U-\lambda_nI)
$$

send $V_n$ into zero, one step in the flag at a time. But

$$
\chi_U(t)=\prod_{j=1}^n(t-\lambda_j),
$$

hence $\chi_U(U)=0$. Similarity gives $\chi_A(A)=0$. For a matrix defined over an arbitrary field $K$, the identity proved over an algebraic closure has entries in $K$ and is therefore true over $K$ itself. $\square$

### A second adjugate viewpoint

The coefficient argument can also be expressed using the commutative algebra $K[T]$ generated by an endomorphism $T$. Scalar multiplication and $T$ commute in this algebra, so the matrix $TI-A$ (where $A$ is the coordinate matrix of $T$) has a valid adjugate. Its action on the column of basis vectors is zero; multiplying by its adjugate gives $\chi_A(T)$ acting as zero on every basis vector. This is a coordinate-free-leaning reformulation of the preceding computation, and explains why commutativity of the polynomial algebra is crucial.

## 6.3.3 — An explicit non-diagonal calculation

**Example 6.3.4.** Take

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\chi_A(t)=t^2-4t+1.
$$

Since

$$
A^2=\begin{pmatrix}7&12\\4&7\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
4A-I=\begin{pmatrix}7&12\\4&7\end{pmatrix},
$$

we conclude that

$$
A^2-4A+I=0.
$$

**Example 6.3.5 (Rotation by $\pi/3$).** The real rotation matrix

$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&\frac12\end{pmatrix}
$$

has characteristic polynomial $t^2-t+1$. Thus

$$
R^2-R+I=0,
$$

which has the geometric interpretation that the difference of rotations by $2\pi/3$ and $\pi/3$ equals the negative identity.

## 6.3.4 — Existence of a minimal polynomial

**Definition 6.3.6.** The **minimal polynomial** $m_A(t)$ is the monic polynomial of smallest degree for which

$$
\boxed{m_A(A)=0.}
$$

The zero polynomial is excluded. For the zero operator on a nonzero space, $m_A(t)=t$; for the identity operator, $m_I(t)=t-1$.

**Proposition 6.3.7 (Existence and uniqueness).** A minimal polynomial exists and is unique.

*Existence via Cayley–Hamilton.* The characteristic polynomial is a monic annihilating polynomial of positive degree, so there exists at least one such polynomial and a monic one of minimum degree.

*Alternative existence proof.* The algebra $M_n(K)$ has dimension $n^2$ over $K$, so the $n^2+1$ matrices

$$
I,A,A^2,\ldots,A^{n^2}
$$

are linearly dependent. A nonzero polynomial annihilates $A$; after dividing by its leading coefficient it becomes monic.

*Uniqueness* follows from the divisibility theorem below: two monic annihilating polynomials of least degree must divide each other, and hence be equal. $\square$

## 6.3.5 — Divisibility and the minimal polynomial

**Theorem 6.3.8 (Divisibility property).** If $f(t)\in K[t]$ satisfies $f(A)=0$, then

$$
\boxed{m_A(t)\mid f(t).}
$$

*Proof.* Divide $f$ by the monic minimal polynomial in $K[t]$:

$$
f(t)=q(t)m_A(t)+r(t),\qquad \deg r<\deg m_A
$$

(or $r=0$). Evaluation at $A$ yields

$$
0=f(A)=q(A)m_A(A)+r(A)=r(A).
$$

If $r\neq0$, normalising its leading coefficient gives an annihilating monic polynomial of strictly smaller degree, contradicting minimality. Thus $r=0$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.3.9.** Cayley–Hamilton implies

$$
\boxed{m_A(t)\mid\chi_A(t),\qquad 1\le\deg m_A\le n.}
$$

Moreover, the roots of $m_A$ in a splitting field are **exactly** the distinct eigenvalues of $A$.

*Proof.* If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue and $m_A(A)=0$, apply both sides to a nonzero eigenvector $v$:

$$
0=m_A(A)v=m_A(\lambda)v.
$$

Hence $m_A(\lambda)=0$. Conversely, every root of $m_A$ is a root of $\chi_A$ because $m_A$ divides $\chi_A$, and thus an eigenvalue. $\square$

**Proposition 6.3.10 (Similarity invariance).** If $B=P^{-1}AP$, then

$$
p(B)=P^{-1}p(A)P
$$

for every polynomial $p$. Consequently $m_B=m_A$ and $\chi_B=\chi_A$. In particular, the minimal polynomial is an invariant of the operator rather than its coordinate matrix.

## 6.3.6 — Relation to diagonalisation

**Theorem 6.3.11 (Square-free minimal polynomial criterion).** An operator is diagonalisable over $K$ if and only if its minimal polynomial splits into **distinct linear factors** over $K:

$$
\boxed{
T\text{ diagonalisable over }K
\iff
m_T(t)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}(t-\lambda_j),\quad \lambda_i\neq\lambda_j\ (i\neq j).
}
$$

*Proof (forward direction).* If $T$ is diagonal in an eigenbasis, the polynomial

$$
q(t)=\prod_{j=1}^s(t-\lambda_j)
$$

vanishes at every diagonal entry, hence $q(T)=0$. Every eigenvalue is a root of $m_T$, so $m_T=q$.

*Proof (converse direction).* Since the factors $(t-\lambda_j)$ are pairwise coprime, the Chinese remainder theorem for polynomials supplies polynomials $e_j(t)$ satisfying

$$
e_j(t)\equiv1\pmod{t-\lambda_j},\qquad
e_j(t)\equiv0\pmod{t-\lambda_k}\quad(k\neq j),
$$

and $\sum_j e_j(t)\equiv1\pmod{m_T(t)}$. Evaluating at $T$, the maps $e_j(T)$ are mutually orthogonal projections summing to the identity, with image in $\ker(T-\lambda_jI)$. Hence

$$
V=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}\ker(T-\lambda_jI),
$$

so a basis of eigenvectors exists. $\square$

An important consequence is that the characteristic polynomial may have repeated factors even when the minimal polynomial does not. For example,

$$
A=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,0),\qquad
\chi_A(t)=(t-2)^2t,\quad m_A(t)=t(t-2).
$$

The matrix is diagonalisable despite the repeated characteristic root $2$.

## 6.3.7 — Examples of minimal polynomials

**Example 6.3.12 (A single Jordan block).** Let

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&1\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad N=J-2I.
$$

Since $N^3=0$ but $N^2\neq0$,

$$
\chi_J(t)=(t-2)^3,\qquad m_J(t)=(t-2)^3.
$$

The repeated factor in $m_J$ witnesses the obstruction to diagonalisation.

**Example 6.3.13 (Projection).** If $P^2=P$ with $P\neq0,I$, then

$$
P(P-I)=0,
$$

so $m_P(t)$ divides $t(t-1)$. Because both $0$ and $1$ occur as eigenvalues for a nontrivial projection, actually

$$
m_P(t)=t(t-1).
$$

Thus every projection is diagonalisable over any field, since $t$ and $t-1$ are distinct factors.

**Example 6.3.14 (Nilpotent operator).** If $N^r=0$ but $N^{r-1}\neq0$, then

$$
m_N(t)=t^r.
$$

In particular, a nonzero nilpotent matrix is never diagonalisable: its minimal polynomial has a repeated root.

**Example 6.3.15 (Differentiation).** On the space $P_d(K)$ of polynomials of degree at most $d$, suppose $K$ has characteristic zero and let $D=d/dx$. Then

$$
D^{d+1}=0,\qquad D^d(x^d)=d!\neq0,
$$

so

$$
m_D(t)=t^{d+1}.
$$

The restriction on characteristic is important: over a field of positive characteristic this conclusion can change.

## 6.3.8 — What the minimal polynomial cannot distinguish

The characteristic polynomial remembers **the total multiplicity** of each eigenvalue. The minimal polynomial instead remembers the **largest length of a Jordan chain** for each eigenvalue (to be proved in §6.4). Neither polynomial by itself always determines similarity.

For instance,

$$
A=\operatorname{diag}(J_2(0),J_2(0)),\qquad
B=\operatorname{diag}(J_2(0),0,0)
$$

are $4\times4$ matrices with

$$
\chi_A(t)=\chi_B(t)=t^4,\qquad
m_A(t)=m_B(t)=t^2,
$$

but $\dim\ker A=2$, whereas $\dim\ker B=3$, so they are not similar. (Here $J_2(0)=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{psmallmatrix}$.)

## 6.3.9 — Reducing high powers and finding inverses

Cayley–Hamilton yields the recurrence

$$
A^n=-a_{n-1}A^{n-1}-\cdots-a_1A-a_0I,
$$

which reduces any high power of $A$ to a linear combination of $I,A,\ldots,A^{n-1}$. Reducing modulo the **minimal** polynomial often gives an even shorter expression.

When $A$ is invertible, $a_0=(-1)^n\det(A)\neq0$. Multiply the Cayley–Hamilton identity by $A^{-1}$ to obtain

$$
\boxed{
A^{-1}=-\frac{1}{a_0}\big(A^{n-1}+a_{n-1}A^{n-2}+\cdots+a_1I\big).
}
$$

This is another theoretical expression for the inverse, alongside the adjugate formula from the determinants chapter.

**Example 6.3.16.** If $A$ from Example 6.3.4 satisfies $A^2=4A-I$, then

$$
A^{-1}=4I-A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-3\\-1&2\end{pmatrix},
$$

and

$$
A^3=A(4A-I)=4(4A-I)-A=15A-4I.
$$
